Can You Sell Medicines in Nigeria Without Local Regulatory Support?

Local regulatory representation is not a box to tick on the way in. It is a business decision that shapes cost, speed and risk for as long as your product is on the market.

By MedNova Lifesciences Published: 11 October 2026 ~6 min read Market Entry
The short answerNo. A foreign manufacturer cannot register or sell a medicine in Nigeria on its own: NAFDAC requires a Nigerian entity to hold the product registration, and that local partner carries real legal responsibility for the product for as long as it is on the market.

Nigeria is one of Africa's largest pharmaceutical markets, with a population of more than 200 million and a growing private healthcare sector. For international manufacturers, it is an obvious place to expand. But many companies treat local regulatory support as a box to tick on the way in, rather than a business decision that shapes cost, speed and risk for years. This post explains why that local role matters, what it really covers, and what can go wrong when it is handled poorly.

Why Nigeria requires a local presence

Every medicine sold in Nigeria needs a NAFDAC registration number. Foreign manufacturers cannot apply for one directly. They must register through a Nigerian company or person, and the registration certificate is issued in that local representative's name (BusinessDay).

In practice, foreign manufacturers usually choose one of three routes:

Route 1

A Nigerian subsidiary

Set up a Nigerian subsidiary that holds the registrations in its own name.

Route 2

Your distributor as local representative

Appoint an existing distributor as local representative.

Route 3

An independent regulatory agent

Appoint an independent regulatory agent that holds registrations separately from commercial distribution.

Whichever route you take, the manufacturer grants the local representative a notarised Power of Attorney to act on its behalf before NAFDAC (Legal500). That document is the legal link between your company and your product's right to be sold in Nigeria.

The business implicationWhoever holds your registration holds significant leverage over your market access.

Registration is the start, not the finish

Most of the local representative's work happens after the registration number is issued. A product stays on the Nigerian market only while these obligations are met:

ResponsibilityWhat it means for your business
PharmacovigilanceAdverse events in Nigeria must be collected, assessed and reported to NAFDAC on time. Gaps here can trigger regulatory action.
RenewalsRegistrations expire and must be renewed before lapse. A missed renewal means product cannot legally be imported or sold.
VariationsChanges to manufacturing site, formulation, labelling or shelf life need NAFDAC approval before they apply to Nigerian stock.
Import permitsEach shipment depends on valid permits tied to the registration holder.
Recalls and quality issuesThe local representative is NAFDAC's first point of contact if a product is found to be substandard, falsified or unsafe.
Regulatory changeNAFDAC guidelines evolve. Someone on the ground must track updates and act on them.

If your local partner treats these tasks as administrative afterthoughts, the commercial consequences land on you: stock held at port, delayed launches, or products pulled from shelves. Registration considerations that shape your timeline

You don't need to master the registration manual, but a few factors drive how long market entry takes and what it costs:

  • GMP inspection. NAFDAC expects the manufacturer to invite its officials to inspect the production site abroad, and approval depends partly on that inspection (Legal500). Scheduling and travel add time and cost.
  • Dossier quality. Incomplete or inaccurate documents are a leading cause of delay (Mondaq). A partner who knows NAFDAC's expectations can catch problems before submission.
  • Supporting documents. Items such as a Certificate of Free Sale from the country of origin and notarised legal documents take time to obtain and authenticate.
  • Agency capacity. Timelines can also be affected by factors outside your control, including industrial action at the agency (Legal500).

Common market-entry risks

The problems foreign manufacturers face in Nigeria are rarely about the science. They are usually about structure and control.

  • Distributor lock-in. Naming your distributor as registration holder is convenient, but it ties your market access to one commercial relationship. If that relationship ends, you must revoke the Power of Attorney, appoint a new representative and notify NAFDAC before the new partner can act (BusinessDay). That transition can disrupt supply.
  • Weak post-approval compliance. Late safety reports, missed renewals or unapproved changes can put registrations at risk, even years after a successful launch.
  • Falsified and substandard products. Nigeria actively fights counterfeit medicines. Brands without strong local monitoring and quick recall capability face reputational damage that is hard to reverse.
  • Unrealistic timelines. Launch plans built on optimistic approval dates lead to wasted marketing spend and strained partner relationships.
  • Policy shifts. Government priorities, including support for local manufacturing, can change the competitive landscape for imported products. Without local insight, these shifts arrive as surprises.

How to choose the right local partner

The right local regulatory partner protects your registrations as company assets. Before you sign, ask:

Questions to ask a local regulatory partner

  • Independence: Is regulatory representation separate from distribution, so you can change distributors without losing market access?
  • Track record: How many products have they registered and maintained with NAFDAC, and in which therapeutic areas?
  • Pharmacovigilance capability: Do they have qualified people and systems to manage safety reporting in Nigeria?
  • Transparency: Will you have full visibility of submissions, correspondence and renewal dates?
  • Exit terms: Does the agreement make it straightforward to transfer registrations if the relationship ends?
  • Regional reach: If Nigeria is your first step in West Africa, can the same partner support entry into neighbouring markets?
